§ 1Why this is a company-level risk

As seller of record, Dodo is a party to every transaction — so the chargeback, the rights-holder claim and the regulator all arrive at Dodo, not at the merchant, who is by then unreachable. And because merchants transact under shared Dodo merchant identifiers, one bad actor's dispute ratio pollutes a pool every honest merchant depends on. The cost of a bad merchant is not bounded by their volume. It is bounded by our standing with our acquirers.

The counterweight matters just as much. Our customer is the solo developer with a two-week-old domain, no company registration and possibly a Stripe ban in their history — indistinguishable from a fraudster on every naive feature. A system tuned only to avoid bad merchants converges on rejecting everyone, and an MoR that rejects everyone has no business.

So the objective is not “catch fraud.” It is: maximise approved legitimate volume within a bounded expected-loss and dispute-ratio budget, and get faster and more confident at the boundary over time.

Problem statement

Dodo assumes full seller-of-record liability but decides from isolated point-in-time evidence, using human judgement that does not persist, against outcomes that arrive months later and are never fed back. The same losses recur, and the reasoning behind a rejection lives in one analyst's head.

We need to turn every merchant decision, incident and outcome into durable, connected, retrievable memory — so each new decision carries everything the platform has learned, and so adverse events measurably strengthen future decisions rather than merely being survived.

§ 2The three failure modes

If the built system doesn't visibly change the outcome of these, it hasn't solved the problem. Names are illustrative.

A — The rights they never had

Undisclosed illegality at intake · the copyright case

An “AI reading companion” with a 40,000-book catalogue on a $9/month subscription. Clean site, working demo, restricted but permitted category. Approved in four minutes. On day 74 a publisher's counsel writes to Dodo, because Dodo is the seller on the receipt.

Knowable at day 0: no licences were ever requested, and the terms page was copied verbatim from a merchant we terminated in March.

B — The product that became a different product

Post-approval drift · the misrepresentation case

Underwritten accurately as B2B meeting summarisation. At month five the team pivots the same domain and the same Dodo account to a prohibited category. Nothing in our records changes. Disputes triple.

The structural gap: underwriting is treated as an event, but merchant risk is a time series. The first signal we receive is the chargeback — the loss itself, arriving as the alert.

C — The same people, the fourth time

Recidivism and rings · the network case

Terminated in March, reapplies in June as a new company, new domain, new director on paper. Read on its own terms the application is clean, and it is approved. Same payout account at one remove, same site template, same beneficial owner behind a nominee.

Why no per-application model catches it: the disqualifying fact is not a property of the applicant. It is a relationship to something we already know.

2.1  The wider taxonomy — and two blind spots

Those three came from worked examples, not a sweep. Read against acquirer-side fraud literature, Visa’s monitoring rules and current FTC enforcement, they sit in only two of four postures a merchant can take — and the other two are not adversarial at all.

PostureCategoriesSignal it needsCovered
Deceiving usUndisclosed illegality (A) · recidivist rings (C) · transaction laundering · merchant identity fraud · bust-outContext graph + volume against underwritten forecastpartly

The gaps are not random. Every one is a financial or behavioural signal rather than a content one, and everything §3 proposes is content-oriented — crawl the site, extract facts, diff the offering. That catches a merchant lying or drifting; it is blind to one that is failing or being attacked. The fix is one more input lane feeding the same graph, not a second system.

Two deserve naming out loud. Deceptive billing is probably the highest-probability risk in Dodo’s actual customer base, because the merchant and the product are entirely real and only the cancellation flow offends — and the FTC has pursued a merchant of record directly on those grounds, with penalties assessed per affected customer. Insolvency is not fraud at all: an honest SaaS merchant goes under owing twelve months of prepaid service, and the refunds land on Dodo. No amount of fraud detection will ever surface it.

§ 3Why a memory layer and a context graph

  • The signal is relational. A classifier consuming an application as a feature vector structurally cannot represent Case C. A graph makes it a two-hop query.
  • There will never be enough labels for supervised learning alone. Confirmed-bad merchants are rare events — dozens, not thousands. That is retrieval-and-precedent territory.
  • Reasoning has to be defensible. “The model scored you 0.83” cannot be defended to a merchant or an acquirer. “Three prior merchants with this fulfilment pattern and rights posture were terminated — here are the cases” can.
  • The threat is adversarial and non-stationary. A quarterly-retrained model always fights the last war; a memory that admits a case the day it is confirmed adapts in hours.

What we take from Mem0 — and what we don't

Transferable: LLM-driven extraction of atomic facts, a hybrid vector-plus-graph store, and an explicit add / update / invalidate lifecycle so memory is reconciled rather than appended. Not transferable: the subject. Mem0 remembers preferences, where being wrong is a mild annoyance and there is no adversary. We would store adverse findings about named individuals who are actively trying to defeat the memory, under erasure rights. So every memory of ours carries provenance, confidence, decay, contestability and supersession — which is why I'd borrow the ideas but own the store.

§ 4How the memory keeps learning

Two update paths, running at very different speeds.

Fast — every case, every day. Each case conversation and each correction an analyst makes is extracted into atomic facts and reconciled against what memory already holds: add if new, update if it refines something, invalidate if it contradicts something, no-op if already known. The next case reads the updated memory minutes later. There is no training step and nothing to schedule — the memory improves as a side effect of being used. This is the loop that matters day to day, and it is buildable with what we already have.

Slow — on confirmed outcome. Terminations, rights claims and dispute breaches arrive 30–180 days after the decision they judge. They are few and authoritative, and they are what we score the memory against.

One risk to design for rather than debate: a memory that updates itself on every interaction also drifts on every interaction, and in risk work the drift direction is always toward paranoia. A fact written with false confidence propagates into every future decision and is much harder to spot than a bad one-off judgement. So the slow path carries a periodic health check.

Health check — and the anti-fragility claim

After ingesting confirmed outcomes, replay the historical decision set. Memory is healthy if it improves on cases it previously got wrong, without degrading cases it previously got right.

Concretely: the Vellum Reader rights claim should, on replay, cause the system to have flagged Lumen Labs — and should not cause it to start declining the eleven legitimate e-book merchants. That is also the number that goes up when we feed the system a disaster, which is what makes “anti-fragile” a measurement rather than a word.

What we are explicitly not building: a policy optimised against a reward signal by trial and error. Rewards here arrive months late, we never observe the counterfactual for merchants we decline, and “exploration” would mean deliberately approving merchants we believe are bad. Nothing in the loop above needs it — continuous memory update plus human feedback gets us the relearning behaviour without that machinery.

§ 5Decisions taken

QuestionDecisionConsequence
DeliverableDemo / evaluation buildOptimise for a visible, legible mechanism over production hardening.
DataSynthetic corpusAuthored merchant population with planted rings and drift cases — so we own the ground truth and the replay test is exactly measurable.
ScopeOnboarding + driftAll three cases in scope. Buyer-side card fraud explicitly out — different system, sub-second budget.
AuthorityAdvisory copilotEvidence-first briefs; a human decides, corrects and confirms. Rationale and feedback capture is a feature, not an afterthought — it is the fast learning path in §4.

Build sequence

  1. Author the corpus and resolve the graph. Measure recidivist-link recall on the planted rings — the cheapest proof the premise holds.
  2. The copilot. Assemble evidence, retrieve similar historical cases with outcomes, produce a brief — findings and precedent first, recommendation last, to counter automation bias. Capture the rationale.
  3. Drift. Re-observe merchant sites and fulfilment channels; alert when observed_selling diverges from claims_to_sell across a policy boundary.
  4. Continuous update, with a health check. Memory reconciles on every case and every piece of feedback. Periodically, replay confirmed outcomes over the historical decision set to confirm it is still improving without regressing — that chart is the anti-fragility claim.

§ 6Working assumptions — every open question, answered

Rather than block on founder input, every question is answered below with an assumed value so the build can proceed. All numbers in this section are assumed, not measured. They are chosen to be plausible for an MoR of Dodo’s stage and stated explicitly so they can be corrected rather than debated in the abstract. Correct any of them and I will re-derive what depends on it.

That is far below the roughly 200 labelled outcomes supervised learning would need, which settles the design: retrieval and precedent, not a trained classifier. 6.2  The operating point, derived

Assumed cost ratio — sets every threshold in the system

Wrongly approving one bad merchant ≈ $25,000 — refunds and chargebacks on volume processed before termination, dispute fees, scheme fines, staff time. Tail cases (a rights claim, regulatory contact) run into six figures; this is the median, not the worst.

Wrongly declining one good merchant ≈ $4,000 — roughly 24 months of lifetime margin on a small SaaS or indie account, plus a reputational cost that is real in a community where founders write publicly about being debanked.

That is a ratio of about 6 : 1, which puts the indifference point at P(bad) ≈ 14%. We should decline only when we believe there is better than a one-in-seven chance the merchant is bad — and be willing to accept roughly six wrongful declines to prevent one wrongful approval. That is far less paranoid than intuition suggests, and it is the single most useful output of writing these assumptions down.

§ 7Structural assumptions — correct these first

A-1
Merchants transact under Dodo's MIDs, so dispute ratios aggregate at platform level. See question 4.
A-2
Onboarding is substantially manual today, and rationales are not captured in structured form.
A-3
Dodo's published merchant acceptance policy — accepted, restricted, prohibited — is the authoritative taxonomy we classify against.
A-4
Continuously crawling merchant sites and public fulfilment channels is contractually acceptable. Drift detection is not buildable otherwise.
A-5
Seconds-to-minutes decision latency is acceptable; this is not an inline checkout path.
A-6
Transaction, refund, payout and login telemetry can be fed into the same graph. §2.1 shows half the risk taxonomy is invisible without it.
